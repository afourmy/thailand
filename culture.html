Culture

Social harmony

เกรงใจ. A mix of consideration, deference, and reluctance to impose on someone or cause them discomfort, even at your own expense. Declining a favour you actually need, because the other person would have to go out of their way, is เกรงใจ. It is also why someone may answer yes while meaning no: refusing directly would impose the discomfort of a refusal.

ใจเย็น, "cool heart". Composure treated as a moral virtue. Losing your temper in public lowers your own standing, not that of the person who wronged you. The opposite, ใจร้อน ("hot heart"), is a criticism. Thai builds hundreds of compounds on ใจ (heart) to describe character and emotion.

ไม่เป็นไร. "Never mind", "it's nothing": a reflex of letting go rather than assigning blame, rooted in Buddhist acceptance, and used even when something objectively bad has just happened. It keeps small incidents from becoming conflicts.

น้ำใจ, "water of the heart". Spontaneous generosity: small kindnesses done without expecting anything in return. มีน้ำใจ is a compliment; ไม่มีน้ำใจ is a criticism.

กาลเทศะ. The sense of what is proper for the time, the place, and the person: how to dress, how to speak, and how to hold your body relative to someone's status. Saying that a person has กาลเทศะ means they behave appropriately in each situation.

สัมมาคารวะ. Knowing how to show proper respect to elders and superiors, in speech, posture, and manner. Like กาลเทศะ, it appears in judgments of character: เด็กคนนี้มีสัมมาคารวะ praises a child for treating elders correctly.

เสียหน้า / รักษาหน้า, losing and saving face. หน้า (face) is social standing made visible. Criticism is delivered indirectly, mistakes are smoothed over, and confrontation is avoided so that nobody loses face in front of others.

Buddhism, karma, and acceptance

บุญ, merit. A daily-life activity and a social currency. ทำบุญ (making merit) covers donating to temples, feeding monks on their morning round, releasing fish or birds, and sponsoring ceremonies. Merit made in this life shapes the next one.

ศีลห้า, the five precepts. ศีล is moral conduct, the precepts a Buddhist undertakes. The basic five:

  • ไม่ฆ่าสัตว์: not to kill any living being.
  • ไม่ลักทรัพย์: not to steal.
  • ไม่ประพฤติผิดในกาม: no sexual misconduct.
  • ไม่พูดปด: not to lie. The Pali word survives in everyday speech: พูดมุสา.
  • ไม่ดื่มสุรา: no alcohol or intoxicants.

Keeping the precepts is ถือศีล or รักษาศีล; breaking one is ผิดศีล. The precepts are numbered, and the numbers are used in conversation: ผิดศีลข้อสาม (breaking precept three) is a standard way of saying that someone cheated on their partner. The compound ศีลธรรม is the ordinary word for morality.

บวช, to ordain. Traditionally every Thai man ordains as a monk once in his life, historically for a full rains retreat, today often for a week or two; leaving the monkhood (สึก) carries no stigma. Ordaining is understood as repaying the บุญคุณ of one's parents: a mother whose son ordains ได้เกาะชายผ้าเหลือง, "gets to hold the hem of the yellow robe", which carries her toward heaven. A man who has been through the monkhood and left carries the title ทิด before his name.

พระ, monks. The language keeps a separate register for monks. A monk ฉัน (eats) rather than กิน and จำวัด (sleeps) rather than นอน; a layperson ถวาย (offers) to a monk rather than ให้ (gives), and นิมนต์ (invites) him rather than เชิญ. Monks are counted with their own classifier, รูป.

เณร. Short for สามเณร: a novice, a boy under twenty ordained on ten precepts. Temples fill with boy novices during the school break (บวชเณรภาคฤดูร้อน), and for generations entering the temple as a เณร was a poor boy's path to an education.

แม่ชี. Women who leave ordinary life for the temple: head and eyebrows shaved, white robes, eight or ten precepts, living at a temple or a nunnery (สำนักชี). A แม่ชี is not an ordained member of the sangha: the Thai monastic order does not recognize full ordination for women (ภิกษุณี), so she stands outside the monkhood and its status; a แม่ชี bows to a monk, never the reverse.

เข้าพรรษา, the rains retreat. Often translated as Buddhist Lent: the three months of the rainy season, roughly July to October, when monks must spend every night in their own temple. พรรษา is the retreat itself, and the unit of a monk's seniority: a monk of ten พรรษา has spent ten rains in the monkhood. Laypeople bring large candles (เทียนพรรษา) to the temples at the start, and many give up alcohol for the whole period (งดเหล้าเข้าพรรษา). The retreat ends with ออกพรรษา.

กรวดน้ำ. Merit can be transferred. After making merit, one slowly pours water while the monks chant, dedicating the merit (อุทิศส่วนกุศล) to deceased relatives and to เจ้ากรรมนายเวร, one's karmic creditors: the beings one has wronged in this life and in past ones.

ปฏิบัติธรรม. To practice the Dhamma: spending a few days at a temple or meditation center, dressed in white (นุ่งขาวห่มขาว), keeping the eight precepts, alternating sitting meditation (นั่งสมาธิ) and walking meditation (เดินจงกรม). It is the standard prescription for a life crisis: someone grieving, heartbroken, or stressed is told to ไปปฏิบัติธรรม.

กรรม, karma. Used in everyday speech: the sigh เวรกรรม when misfortune strikes frames bad luck as the ripening of past deeds, one's own or someone else's.

เวร. Where กรรม is the deed and its fruit, เวร is the tie of hatred with the being you wronged, which keeps ripening as retribution across lives; it is the second half of เวรกรรม and of เจ้ากรรมนายเวร. จองเวร is to hold the grudge and keep the cycle going; the Dhammapada line เวรย่อมระงับด้วยการไม่จองเวร, "hatred is ended by not holding on to hatred", is about breaking it. In everyday speech เวร serves as a swear word when something goes wrong, and ไอ้เวร as an insult. A separate word spelled the same way means a duty shift: อยู่เวร, to be on duty, and ทำเวร, the classroom cleaning duty at school.

กรรมเก่า, old karma. The belief that suffering in this life is the fruit of deeds in a past life (ชาติที่แล้ว): someone born blind is assumed to have destroyed eyes in a former one, poverty or chronic illness point to old debts of the same kind. The remedy is แก้กรรม, "fixing karma" through prescribed merit-making. A modern form of it is สแกนกรรม ("karma scanning", from the English word): fortune tellers who diagnose exactly which past deed caused which affliction, and which merit will undo it.

ปลง. To accept something with detachment and stop struggling against what cannot be changed: the inner act of putting a burden down.

ทำใจ, "to make one's heart". To prepare your heart to accept a loss or a disappointment. Before delivering bad news, a speaker may tell you to ทำใจ first.

ดวง. One's fortune or astrological luck, treated as an active force. People check whether their ดวง is up or down before big decisions; ดวงดี / ดวงตก (good fortune / fallen fortune) are everyday diagnoses.

บารมี. Accumulated moral authority and charisma: the influence of a person whose past merit gives them legitimate standing over others. It is the basis of the authority of leaders, patrons, and respected elders.

The calendar

พ.ศ., the Buddhist Era. Thailand counts years from the passing of the Buddha: พุทธศักราช = CE + 543, so 2026 is พ.ศ. 2569. This is the year on official documents, newspapers, coins, and expiry dates. It became the official era in 1913 under Rama VI; older records use จุลศักราช, the era of the Ayutthaya chronicles counted from 638 CE, and briefly รัตนโกสินทรศก, counted from the founding of Bangkok in 1782.

Three new years. Thailand celebrates the new year three times. Two come from the history of the official date: the year traditionally began in April, at สงกรานต์, was fixed to 1 April in 1889, then moved to 1 January (ปีใหม่) in 1941, a change that left the year พ.ศ. 2483 only nine months long. The old date was never abandoned: สงกรานต์ remains the biggest holiday of the year. The third, ตรุษจีน (Chinese New Year), owes its place not to the calendar but to the country's large Chinese-Thai population.

Two calendars in parallel. Civil life runs on the solar calendar (สุริยคติ); religion runs on the lunar one (จันทรคติ). Lunar months are numbered, the first (เดือนอ้าย) falling around December and the second called เดือนยี่, and days are counted by the moon: ขึ้น ๘ ค่ำ is the eighth night of the waxing moon, แรม ๘ ค่ำ the eighth of the waning. Every major religious date is a full moon of some numbered month.

วันพระ, holy days. The lunar holy days, four per month (the eighth night and the last night of each waxing and waning half-month), when practicing Buddhists go to the temple and some take the eight precepts (ถือศีลแปด). On the major holidays (วิสาขบูชา, มาฆบูชา, อาสาฬหบูชา, and the days of เข้าพรรษา and ออกพรรษา) alcohol sales are banned nationwide, and the evening ends with เวียนเทียน: three candle-lit circuits around the temple hall.

ปีนักษัตร, the animal years. The twelve-year animal cycle, from ปีชวด (rat) to ปีกุน (pig). Since the age ladder decides how people address each other, asking someone's animal year is a polite way of establishing exactly how much older or younger they are: two people of the same animal are twelve years apart or exactly the same age.

Birthday colors. Each day of the week has a color and its own Buddha image (พระประจำวันเกิด), and Thais know which weekday they were born on. The yellow that floods the country on royal birthdays is Monday's color, the birth day of both Rama IX and Rama X.

The year, January to December.

  • วันขึ้นปีใหม่ (1 January): the year opens with morning alms at the temple.
  • ตรุษจีน (January or February): Chinese New Year, not an official holiday but widely celebrated.
  • มาฆบูชา (full moon of the third lunar month, February or March): commemorates the day 1,250 disciples assembled around the Buddha without being summoned.
  • สงกรานต์ (13 to 15 April): the traditional new year, when the country goes home to its provinces. The water fights grew out of quieter gestures that still happen: pouring scented water over Buddha images (สรงน้ำพระ) and over the hands of elders (รดน้ำดำหัว).
  • วันพืชมงคล (May, date set by court astrologers): the royal ploughing ceremony that opens the rice season; the royal oxen are offered foods, and their choice predicts the harvest.
  • วิสาขบูชา (full moon of the sixth month, May): the Buddha's birth, awakening, and passing, all commemorated on the same day.
  • อาสาฬหบูชา (full moon of the eighth month, July): the first sermon. The next day เข้าพรรษา begins, with candle processions (แห่เทียนพรรษา), grandest in Ubon Ratchathani.
  • วันแม่ (12 August): Mother's Day, the birthday of Queen Sirikit.
  • สารทไทย (end of the tenth month, September or October): merit-making for dead ancestors, with the caramel-rice sweet กระยาสารท.
  • กินเจ (nine days in late September or October): the Chinese-Thai vegetarian festival, yellow flags over every market, at its most spectacular in Phuket.
  • ออกพรรษา (full moon of the eleventh month, October): the retreat ends. The next morning, ตักบาตรเทโว alms commemorate the Buddha's descent from the heaven where he spent the retreat teaching his mother; the same night, crowds in Nong Khai watch the บั้งไฟพญานาค, fireballs rising from the Mekong attributed to the Naga. The month that follows is the กฐิน season, when robes are formally presented to each temple.
  • ลอยกระทง (full moon of the twelfth month, November): banana-trunk floats with a candle, incense, and flowers are set on the water, honoring and asking forgiveness of the water goddess พระแม่คงคา. In the North, ยี่เป็ง fills the sky with lanterns (โคมลอย).
  • วันพ่อ (5 December): Father's Day, the birthday of King Rama IX.
  • 31 December: temples hold สวดมนต์ข้ามปี, chanting across midnight into the new year.

Scattered between these are the royal commemorations of the civil calendar: วันจักรี (6 April), the present king's birthday (28 July), and วันปิยมหาราช (23 October), among others.

The spirit world

ผี, spirits. Spirits and ghosts treated as a practical presence in daily life. Buildings have spirit houses (ศาลพระภูมิ) that receive daily offerings of food, drink, and garlands: the displaced spirits of the land need somewhere to live.

ตายโหง. To die a violent, unnatural death: an accident, a murder, a suicide. The spirit of someone who died this way becomes a ผีตายโหง, the strongest and most dangerous kind of ghost, bound to the place of death; shrines appear at spots on the road where accidents keep happening, and a house where someone ตายโหง is hard to sell. The worst case is ตายทั้งกลม, dying with a child in the womb. The word is also used as a curse: ไอ้ตายโหง wishes that death on someone.

Named ghosts. The best-known spirits have names and stories. แม่นาค died in childbirth waiting for her husband and refused to leave; she has a shrine at Wat Mahabut in Bangkok and is retold in film after film. กระสือ is a woman's head that floats through the night with its insides hanging below it, seen from afar as a floating light. ปอบ possesses people and eats them from the inside. นางตะเคียน lives in the takian tree: a trunk wrapped in colored cloth is hers. กุมารทอง, the golden boy, is a child spirit adopted into the household and given daily offerings in exchange for luck.

ขวัญ. The life-essence residing in a person. It can flee the body when you are badly frightened (ขวัญเสีย, ขวัญหาย) and is called back in ceremonies. A gift, ของขวัญ, is literally a thing that strengthens someone's ขวัญ.

ตาทิพย์, "divine eyes". ทิพย์ is divine, celestial: belonging to the world of gods and spirits. ตาทิพย์ is the clairvoyant sight attributed to monks and mediums who can see spirits, past lives, or hidden things; หูทิพย์ is the matching divine ear. It is also used teasingly: a mother who somehow knows everything her children have done has ตาทิพย์.

บนบาน / แก้บน. A bargain struck with a spirit or a shrine: if the wish is granted (a child, an exam, a lottery number), the person who made the wish owes the promised offering. Repaying the vow is แก้บน, and the promised goods range from boiled eggs and red Fanta to pigs' heads and hired classical dancers. Leaving a vow unpaid is considered dangerous.

หวย, the lottery. The government lottery is drawn on the 1st and 16th of every month, and the days before each draw are spent hunting for เลขเด็ด, "sharp numbers": dreams are converted into digits, as are the license plates of crashed cars, oddly shaped trees, and anything a revered monk says by accident. The underground version, หวยใต้ดิน, takes bets on the last two or three digits of the official draw.

พระเครื่อง, amulets. Small Buddha images worn on a neck chain for protection, blessed by revered monks (ปลุกเสก). One never buys an amulet: the verb is เช่า, to rent. There is a collectors' market with dedicated magazines, and famous amulets trade for millions of baht.

The life cycle

Birth. A newborn is not praised as beautiful: visitors say น่าเกลียดน่าชัง ("ugly-adorable") so that no spirit will want to take the child. Infants are watched over by แม่ซื้อ, their spirit guardians; a baby smiling in its sleep is being played with by them.

งานแต่ง, the wedding. The groom's side arrives in a ขันหมาก procession bearing trays of gifts, and must pay its way past ประตูเงินประตูทอง, the "silver and gold gates" held shut by the bride's friends. The สินสอด, the bride price agreed between the families, is counted out and displayed during the ceremony; it repays the parents' ค่าน้ำนม, "the price of milk". Guests bless the couple by pouring water over their hands from a conch shell (รดน้ำสังข์).

งานศพ, the funeral. A funeral lasts days. The body rests at the temple while monks chant สวดพระอภิธรรม each evening (three, five, or seven nights), the hosts feed the guests, and wreaths (พวงหรีด) sent by employers, schoolmates, and associations line the hall. At the cremation, each guest places a ดอกไม้จันทน์ (sandalwood flower) beneath the coffin, and a male relative may ordain for the day (บวชหน้าไฟ) to send merit to the dead. Merit-making follows at 7, 50, and 100 days (ทำบุญร้อยวัน).

Hierarchy and obligation

พี่ / น้อง. Older sibling / younger sibling, used far beyond the family: colleagues, friends, waiters, strangers. Speakers place each other on the age ladder, and the kinship term does the placing.

ผู้ใหญ่ / ผู้น้อย. Literally "big people" and "small people": seniors and juniors in the social order. A ผู้ใหญ่ receives deference and owes protection; a ผู้น้อย owes respect and can expect patronage.

ครับ / ค่ะ. The polite particles that end sentences, chosen by the speaker's gender. The same sentence with and without the particle can be the difference between polite and rude.

ราชาศัพท์. The royal register: a separate vocabulary, largely Khmer and Pali-Sanskrit, used when speaking of or to royalty. The king เสวย (eats), บรรทม (sleeps), เสด็จ (goes), and สวรรคต (dies). A subject addressing him calls themself ข้าพระพุทธเจ้า and addresses ใต้ฝ่าละอองธุลีพระบาท, "the dust under the soles of the royal feet". It is taught at school and heard daily in the royal news.

ไหว้. The palms-together greeting. The junior person initiates it, and the height of the hands follows the status of the person being greeted. Three levels are distinguished:

  • For monks and Buddha images: the thumbs rest between the eyebrows, fingertips at the hairline, with a deep bow of the head.
  • For parents, teachers, and elders: the thumbs rest on the tip of the nose, fingertips between the eyebrows.
  • For equals: the thumbs rest on the chin, fingertips at the nose, with a slight bow.

The senior person does not answer at the same height: they respond with a รับไหว้ (receiving greeting), hands at chest level, or with a nod. Monks do not return the greeting at all. For the highest respect (Buddha images, monks, royalty, one's parents in formal settings), the ไหว้ gives way to the กราบ, a bow to the floor.

หัว / เท้า. The body carries the same ladder: the head is its highest and most sacred point, the feet its lowest. Touching someone's head is deeply rude, and objects are not passed over one. Feet must not point at a person or a Buddha image, rest on a table, or step over (ข้าม) a person, food, or a book. Stepping on a coin to stop it rolling combines the two extremes at once: the king's head is on the coin, under a foot.

ระบบอุปถัมภ์, the patronage system. The network of patron and client that runs through offices, politics, and the army: the ผู้ใหญ่ gives protection, opportunities, and help in trouble, and the ผู้น้อย under their อุปถัมภ์ (patronage) owes loyalty and service in return. Used critically, the word names a system where connections beat merit; having them is มีเส้น, "to have a line".

บุญคุณ. The moral debt created when someone does something significant for you: parents raising you, a teacher forming you, a patron helping you. The debt does not expire, and it constrains what you may do afterwards: criticizing or abandoning someone whose บุญคุณ you carry is a moral failing, not just rudeness. Repaying it is ตอบแทนบุญคุณ. Children supporting their parents, and loyalty toward bosses and patrons, both run on this concept.

กตัญญู. The virtue of recognizing the บุญคุณ one has received and repaying it, taught from the first years of school. Its negation, อกตัญญู (ungrateful), is a severe moral accusation.

ผู้ดี. The ideal of the well-raised person: refined, controlled, considerate conduct. The etiquette manual สมบัติของผู้ดี ("the qualities of a ผู้ดี") codifies it and is still taught in schools. In this word, manners mark social class.

ศักดินา, "power of fields". The ranking system of the Ayutthaya kingdom, codified under King Trailok in the 15th century: every person in the realm was assigned a number expressed in rai of land. A slave held 5, a commoner 25, officials from the hundreds into the thousands, the highest nobles 10,000, and the heir to the throne 100,000; the king stood above the scale. The number determined precedence, the size of fines and compensation, and the severity of punishments in disputes between people of different rank. The system faded with the reforms of the late 19th and early 20th centuries, but the word survives: calling something ศักดินา in modern Thai accuses it of feudal, aristocratic thinking.

Special expressions

รู้จักที่ต่ำที่สูง, "to know the low and the high". To know one's place in the social order. Someone described as ไม่รู้จักที่ต่ำที่สูง has behaved above their station: spoken too familiarly to a superior, contradicted an elder, claimed a seat or a role that was not theirs to claim.

ทำดีได้ดี ทำชั่วได้ชั่ว. "Do good, receive good; do evil, receive evil": karma compressed into a proverb, taught from the first years of school. It has an equally famous cynical answer, ทำดีได้ดีมีที่ไหน ทำชั่วได้ดีมีถมไป: "where does doing good ever pay? doing evil and prospering is everywhere".

ตนเป็นที่พึ่งแห่งตน. "Oneself is one's own refuge", a Dhammapada verse turned into a school-wall motto. Your karma is your own doing, so improvement comes from your own merit and effort, not from petitioning a god.

ปิดทองหลังพระ. "Gilding the back of the Buddha image": doing good where nobody will see it. From the practice of pressing gold-leaf squares onto statues; the front is covered quickly, so real merit goes on the back. Its opposite also has a name: ทำบุญเอาหน้า, making merit to gain face.

มือถือสาก ปากถือศีล. "The hand holds the pestle, the mouth holds the precepts": a hypocrite who preaches virtue while doing wrong. Note the measure of virtue: keeping ศีล.

น้ำท่วมปาก. "Water floods the mouth": knowing something but being unable to say it, because speaking would harm you or someone you must not harm. The proverb for every meeting where the junior person sees the problem and says nothing.

เข้าเมืองตาหลิ่ว ต้องหลิ่วตาตาม. "When you enter the city of squinting people, squint along": adapt to the people around you rather than standing out. Where English "when in Rome" is travel advice, the Thai proverb states a general rule of social life.

เห็นช้างขี้ ขี้ตามช้าง. "Seeing the elephant defecate, defecating like the elephant": trying to live or spend like people far above your means. Usually given as advice with อย่า (don't), and one more expression that assumes everyone has a place on the ladder.

สำเนียงส่อภาษา กิริยาส่อสกุล. "The accent betrays the language, the manners betray the lineage": how you speak and carry yourself reveals where you come from and how you were raised. The proverb behind ผู้ดี: upbringing cannot be faked, and everyone is listening for it.

ช้า ๆ ได้พร้าเล่มงาม. "Slowly, slowly gets a fine machete": forging a good blade cannot be rushed. Patience as craftsmanship, the proverb form of ใจเย็น.

กบในกะลาครอบ. "A frog under a coconut shell": someone whose world is tiny and who therefore thinks they know everything, the shell being the whole of their sky. Said of narrow-mindedness, and built, fittingly, out of a coconut.
