Culture

Culture-bound words. A culture-bound word either names a concept the culture built for itself, or only makes sense once you understand how the society works. In Thai, these words cluster around a few themes: social harmony, Buddhism, the spirit world, pleasure, and hierarchy.

Social harmony

เกรงใจ (kreng jai). A mix of consideration, deference, and reluctance to impose on someone or cause them discomfort, even at your own expense. Declining a favour you actually need, because the other person would have to go out of their way, is เกรงใจ. It is also why someone may answer yes while meaning no: refusing directly would impose the discomfort of a refusal.

ใจเย็น (jai yen), "cool heart". Composure treated as a moral virtue. Losing your temper in public lowers your own standing, not that of the person who wronged you. The opposite, ใจร้อน ("hot heart"), is a criticism. Thai builds hundreds of compounds on ใจ (heart) to describe character and emotion.

ไม่เป็นไร (mai pen rai). "Never mind", "it's nothing": a reflex of letting go rather than assigning blame, rooted in Buddhist acceptance, and used even when something objectively bad has just happened. It keeps small incidents from becoming conflicts.

น้ำใจ (nam jai), "water of the heart". Spontaneous generosity: small kindnesses done without expecting anything in return. มีน้ำใจ (to have น้ำใจ) is a compliment; ไม่มีน้ำใจ (to lack it) is a criticism.

กาลเทศะ (kalathesa). The sense of what is proper for the time, the place, and the person: how to dress, how to speak, and how to hold your body relative to someone's status. Saying that a person has กาลเทศะ means they behave appropriately in each situation.

เสียหน้า / รักษาหน้า, losing and saving face. หน้า (face) is social standing made visible. Criticism is delivered indirectly, mistakes are smoothed over, and confrontation is avoided so that nobody loses face in front of others.

Buddhism, karma, and acceptance

บุญ (bun), merit. A daily-life activity and a social currency. ทำบุญ (making merit) covers donating to temples, feeding monks on their morning round, releasing fish or birds, and sponsoring ceremonies. Merit made in this life shapes the next one.

กรรม (gam), karma. Used in everyday speech: the sigh เวรกรรม when misfortune strikes frames bad luck as the ripening of past deeds, one's own or someone else's.

ปลง (plong). To accept something with detachment and stop struggling against what cannot be changed: the inner act of putting a burden down.

ทำใจ (tham jai), "to make one's heart". To brace yourself into acceptance of a loss or a disappointment. Before delivering bad news, a speaker may tell you to ทำใจ first.

ดวง (duang). One's fortune or astrological luck, treated as an active force. People check whether their ดวง is up or down before big decisions; ดวงดี / ดวงตก (good fortune / fallen fortune) are everyday diagnoses.

บารมี (barami). Accumulated moral authority and charisma: the influence of a person whose past merit gives them legitimate standing over others. It underpins the authority of leaders, patrons, and respected elders.

The spirit world

ผี (phi), spirits. Spirits and ghosts treated as a practical presence in daily life. Buildings have spirit houses (ศาลพระภูมิ) that receive daily offerings of food, drink, and garlands: the displaced spirits of the land need somewhere to live.

ขวัญ (khwan). The life-essence residing in a person. It can flee the body when you are badly frightened (ขวัญเสีย, ขวัญหาย) and is called back in ceremonies. A gift, ของขวัญ, is literally a thing that strengthens someone's ขวัญ.

Pleasure and ease

สนุก (sanuk), fun. A criterion for whether an activity is worth doing. A job can be rejected because it is ไม่สนุก; an event is judged by whether it was สนุก.

สบาย (sabai). Bodily and mental ease, comfort, well-being. Doubled as สบาย ๆ it describes the attitude of taking things easy, without hurry or worry.

เที่ยว (thiao). To go out for pleasure with no particular goal: visiting places, wandering, having a good time (ไปเที่ยว).

Hierarchy built into the language

พี่ / น้อง (phi / nong). Older sibling / younger sibling, used far beyond the family: colleagues, friends, waiters, strangers. Speakers place each other on the age ladder, and the kinship term does the placing.

ผู้ใหญ่ / ผู้น้อย (phu yai / phu noi). Literally "big people" and "small people": seniors and juniors in the social order. A ผู้ใหญ่ receives deference and owes protection; a ผู้น้อย owes respect and can expect patronage.

ครับ / ค่ะ (khrap / kha). The polite particles that end sentences, chosen by the speaker's gender. The same sentence with and without the particle can be the difference between polite and rude.

ไหว้ (wai). The palms-together greeting. The junior person initiates it, and the height of the hands follows the status of the person being greeted. Three levels are distinguished:

  • For monks and Buddha images: the thumbs rest between the eyebrows, fingertips at the hairline, with a deep bow of the head.
  • For parents, teachers, and elders: the thumbs rest on the tip of the nose, fingertips between the eyebrows.
  • For equals: the thumbs rest on the chin, fingertips at the nose, with a slight bow.

The senior person does not wai back at the same height: they answer with a รับไหว้ (receiving wai), hands at chest level, or with a nod. Monks do not return a wai at all. For the highest respect (Buddha images, monks, royalty, one's parents in formal settings), the wai gives way to the กราบ (krap), a bow to the floor.

ศักดินา (sakdina), "power of fields". The ranking system of the Ayutthaya kingdom, codified under King Trailok in the 15th century: every person in the realm was assigned a number expressed in rai of land. A slave held 5, a commoner 25, officials from the hundreds into the thousands, the highest nobles 10,000, and the heir to the throne 100,000; the king stood above the scale. The number determined precedence, the size of fines and compensation, and the severity of punishments in disputes between people of different rank. The system faded with the reforms of the late 19th and early 20th centuries, but the word survives: calling something ศักดินา in modern Thai accuses it of feudal, aristocratic thinking.
